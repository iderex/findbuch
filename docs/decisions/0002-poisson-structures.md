# 0002. The phase space and the Poisson structure are data

Decided in #3.

## The problem

A checker that assumes one Poisson structure will silently verify the wrong
statement about half the catalogue.

The classical heavy body with a fixed point lives on the dual of e(3), with the
bracket generated by the angular momentum and the direction of gravity, and with
the two Casimirs that go with it. The Kirchhoff family, meaning Clebsch,
Steklov-Lyapunov and their relatives, is written on the same six variables but is
a different physical problem, and several important cases in the literature are
stated on so(4) or so(3,1) instead. Gyrostat cases add a constant term that
changes the Hamiltonian but not the bracket, and some of the later literature
uses a bracket deformed by a gyroscopic term where it does change.

If the bracket is written into the checker, then a row transcribed from a
Kirchhoff paper is checked against the wrong bracket. It either fails, or it
passes for the wrong reason. Neither outcome tells the reader anything.

## The decision

The Poisson structure is data, not code. Each structure gets a file that
declares its coordinates, its bracket table between the generators, and its
Casimir functions. Each row names the structure it lives on by identifier. The
checker reads the named structure and computes the bracket from the declared
table, so adding a structure is adding a file rather than editing the engine.

The catalogue starts with three structures: e(3), so(4) and so(3,1). Those are
the ones that cover the general and conditional cases in the surveys.

They are written as one parametrised family with the parameter fixed per file,
not as three unrelated tables. They differ in exactly one sign or coefficient,
and writing them out separately invites the three to drift apart under later
edits.

## The Casimirs are declared and checked, not derived

Deriving the Casimirs would be a second piece of mathematics to get right, and
they are load-bearing: the numeric leg needs them to stay on the correct
symplectic leaf, and their drift is what the numeric verdict is measured
against. So they are stated in the structure file.

A declaration on its own would be dangerous, so it is checked. A test computes
the bracket of each declared Casimir with each generator of its structure and
requires the result to be identically zero. A structure whose Casimir is wrong
is refused at test time rather than producing a numeric noise floor that quietly
means nothing.

The same test covers the bracket table itself: antisymmetry on every pair, and
the Jacobi identity on every triple. Both are cheap on three generators of six
coordinates, and both catch a transposed sign in the table, which is the mistake
that will actually be made.

The declaration is what makes the structures convenient. The test is what makes
them safe. Neither is optional.

## What this rejects

Hard-coding e(3) and adding the others later. The later addition would be a
rewrite of the checker at the point where it is most load-bearing, and a
catalogue that cannot hold Clebsch is not a catalogue of the integrable cases.

Deriving the structure from the Hamiltonian. This is not possible, and it would
not be desirable if it were. The bracket is part of the statement of the
problem, not something recovered from one half of it.

## Consequences

A row that names no structure cannot be checked, so the structure identifier is
required rather than defaulted. A default would be e(3), and e(3) is exactly the
wrong answer for the family most likely to be transcribed carelessly.

Adding a fourth structure later is adding a file and its three tests, and
touches no checker code. That is the property this record exists to protect.
